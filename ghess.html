Ghess

Chess, but on ten by ten, with a camel tucked between each knight and bishop. Everything else is the chess you know — castling, en passant, promotion — except that the camel's pawns are giraffe pawns and can only ever become giraffes. Behind each king sits a citadel: get your king into the other one and you win. Two house rules on top: a checked king may, once a game, trade places with one of its pieces (the shah’s privilege); and each side has a griffin waiting off the board, which from move twenty-five may swoop in and kill a pawn, hunt for two moves, and fly off.

min + s

How Ghess works

Ghess is ordinary chess stretched to a ten-by-ten board, with one piece borrowed from Timur's chess: the camel.

Setting up

White's back rank, from the left: rook, knight, camel, bishop, queen, king, bishop, camel, knight, rook — the queen on e1 and the king on f1. Ten pawns stand in front. Black mirrors it on ranks nine and ten, so the kings face each other on the f-file and the queens on the e-file. White moves first.

Choose 960 in the lobby and the back rank is shuffled instead — the bishops on opposite colours, the king somewhere between the rooks — the same for both sides. Each shuffle has a number, shown as game #N, so a setup can be played again.

Moving

Tap "the pieces" for diagrams. The king, queen, rook, bishop, knight and pawns move exactly as in chess. The camel jumps one square across and three along — a stretched knight — over anything in the way. Like a bishop it always stays on the colour it started on.

Pawns and the giraffe

One square forward, two from the starting rank; captures diagonally; en passant works. A pawn that reaches the far rank promotes — to a queen, rook, bishop or knight, your choice. Except the two giraffe pawns, the ones that start in front of the camels: they wear a giraffe's head, and they can only ever promote to a giraffe. No other pawn can. The giraffe isn't on the board at the start; it moves as Timur's giraffe — one square diagonally, then at least three squares straight on outward, every square on the way empty — and it has the camel's leap of one across and three along.

The citadels

Behind each king's starting square, off the edge of the board, sits a citadel (the dashed ring). Only a king can enter it, and only the enemy king: step into it from any of the three squares in front of it and you win on the spot — even out of check. Your own king can't go in. In a 960 setup the citadels sit behind wherever the kings start.

Castling

As in chess, with the king and rook unmoved, nothing between them, and the king not in, through or into check. The king ends on the c- or i-file and the rook lands beside it on the d- or h-file — in a 960 setup too, wherever they started. Tap the king, then the rook (or the king's landing square).

The shah's privilege

Borrowed from Timur's chess (Murray calls it the king's privilege). Once a game, when your king is in check, instead of moving it you may swap it with one of your pieces — not a pawn: the king goes to that piece's square and the piece to the king's. The swap has to get you out of check like any other move, and castling rights are gone afterwards. Tap the checked king: the pieces it can trade places with get a dashed ring.

The griffin

Each side has one griffin, off the board, shown beside the player's name. From move twenty-five on (once both sides have made twenty-four moves), on your turn you may swoop: the griffin lands on any enemy pawn and kills it. That is its whole turn, and it can only ever come in on a pawn. Tap the griffin beside your name to see the pawns it can take.

It then stays for two of your moves. On each it may move — and capture — one square diagonally, or you may move something else; it gives check like any piece. After your second move it flies away for good (the griffin fades once it has one move left). It comes only once a game.

If the griffin is killed while it's on the board, the pawn it took isn't lost for good: it comes back on the square where the griffin died, as soon as the piece that killed it moves away — a camel pawn comes back as a camel pawn. (If that square happens to be the pawn's last rank, it stays gone.)

Ending

Checkmate wins; so does walking your king into the enemy citadel. Stalemate, bare kings (with no griffin or pawn still to come), fifty moves without a capture or a pawn move, or the same position three times, is a draw. Analysis: tap any move in the list to go back to that position; from there you can move the pieces for either side and the eval bar follows — the game itself is untouched, and "back to the game" returns you to it. When a game is over, tapping the board (or "analyze") opens the final position the same way. The eval bar button shows the computer's opinion of the position beside the board — white's share of the bar, with the score in pawns or a mate distance — recomputed after every move (a second or so of thinking, so take it as a hint, not a verdict). The strip under the board shows what each side has taken and who is ahead, counting the queen 9, rook 5, bishop 3, knight 3, camel 2, giraffe 4, griffin 1 (in hand or on the board), pawn 1. You can also resign or offer a draw. In a timed game each side has the same minutes on the clock and gets the increment back after every move; run out and you lose on time. In a shared game the clocks start with White's first move.
