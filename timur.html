Shatranj al-kabir

The great chess played at Tamerlane's court around 1400: eleven files, ten ranks, two citadels, twenty-eight pieces a side, and a pawn for every kind of piece — each of which promotes into the thing it is named for.

How Timur's chess works

Also called Tamerlane chess, shatranj al-kabir (large chess) or shatranj kamil (perfect chess). The rules here follow Gollon and Murray as summarised by Bodlaender and Wikipedia; where the old sources are silent, this page's choices are marked.

The board

Eleven files (a–k) by ten ranks, uncheckered, plus two citadels: one sticking out left of the ninth rank (Black's), one right of the second rank (White's). Only a king may enter a citadel.

Setting up

The old manuscripts give more than one array; the lobby offers two. Masculine (the usual one) — White from the bottom: rank 1 — elephant, ·, camel, ·, war engine, ·, war engine, ·, camel, ·, elephant (the gaps stay empty). Rank 2 — rook, knight, picket, giraffe, general, king, vizier, giraffe, picket, knight, rook. Rank 3 — the eleven pawns: of pawns, of war engines, of camels, of elephants, of generals, of kings, of viziers, of giraffes, of pickets, of knights, of rooks. Feminine — the king, general and vizier drop to the back rank between the camels (general e1, king f1, vizier g1), the war engines move up to c2 and i2 with the pickets beside them on d2 and h2, and the pawn of kings stands on f2, right in front of the king, leaving f3 empty. Either way Black's army is the same turned through a half-turn, so the kings face each other on the f-file. White moves first.

Moving

Tap "the pieces" for diagrams. Each pawn wears a sign of the piece it will become; the plain pawn is the pawn of pawns. The prince is a smaller crown; the adventitious king is a pawn wearing the crown. In short: the king steps one square any way; the general one square diagonally; the vizier one square straight. The rook and knight are the modern ones. The picket is a bishop that must move at least two squares. The giraffe steps one square diagonally, then at least three squares straight on, with nothing in the way. The elephant jumps exactly two squares diagonally, the war engine exactly two straight, and the camel jumps one-and-three like a stretched knight — none of the three can be blocked. Pawns move one square forward and capture one square diagonally forward; there is no double step, no en passant, no castling.

Promotion

A pawn that reaches the far rank becomes the piece it is named for — the pawn of rooks a rook, the pawn of camels a camel. Two are special. The pawn of kings becomes a prince, which moves like a king and counts as a second king: while you have more than one royal piece you may be captured like anything else and you may even walk into check — only your last royal piece has to be mated.

The pawn of pawns is the strange one. The first time it reaches the far rank it parks there, uncapturable, until a chance comes: whenever a square exists from which it would fork two enemy pieces, or attack a piece that cannot move, you may (on your turn) drop it on that square — even if the square is occupied, by anyone but a king; whatever stood there is removed — and it carries on as a pawn from there. The second time it promotes, it is lifted straight back to the pawn of kings' starting square (f3 / f8 in the masculine array, f2 / f9 in the feminine). The third time, it becomes an adventitious king, another royal piece, and the only one that may sit in its own citadel, where it cannot be taken.

The citadels

If your highest king (king outranks prince outranks adventitious king) can step into the opponent's citadel, the game is a draw — the escape hatch for a losing side. Nothing else may enter either citadel, except your own adventitious king into your own.

The king's privilege

Once in the game, when your king is in check, you may swap it with any one of your other, non-royal pieces instead of moving. Tap the checked king: the pieces it can trade places with get a dashed ring.

Winning

Checkmate wins. So does stalemate: a player who has no legal move loses. Baring the king is nothing. The old game has no repetition or 50-move draws; here, as a house rule, a position seen three times is a draw so games against the computer can end. You can also resign, or offer a draw.
